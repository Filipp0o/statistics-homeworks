How point addition works

For P ≠ Q, λ = (yQ − yP) · (xQ − xP)⁻¹ mod 17. For P = Q, λ = 3xP² · (2yP)⁻¹ mod 17. Then xR = λ² − xP − xQ and yR = λ(xP − xR) − yP, all modulo 17. Division means multiplication by a modular inverse, rather than decimal division.

O + P = P. If Q = −P, P + Q = O. Doubling a point with y = 0 also gives O.

Numerical correlations

Pearson correlation measures only a linear relationship between numbers. A value near zero does not establish independence or security: Q(k + 1) = Q(k) + G remains an exact relationship.

Changing one bit of k

All distinct unordered pairs of valid scalars differing in one bit are enumerated. Hamming distance is measured on the 10 bits of x || y. This test uses the complete valid domain for G, independently of the chart sample. It is an educational observation, not evidence of security.

Interpreting the results

Scalar multiplication is deterministic and preserves group structure. On the canonical domain, k → kG is a bijection onto the nonidentity points of the subgroup. The cryptographic property sought is the difficulty of recovering k from Q: the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem.

For a cryptographic hash, preimage, second-preimage and collision resistance, as well as bit diffusion, are also studied. Avalanche behavior and regular histograms can be useful observations, but they do not prove security. The toy curve is too small to be secure.

Written solution

This exercise demonstrates how a private scalar determines a public point through elliptic-curve addition. The program implements the toy curve, prints the sequence of multiples, animates the calculation and explores the statistical properties of the outputs.

HMWK 1.1 Curve and Scalar Multiplication

y2 ≡ x3 + 7 (mod 17)

The base point is G = (1, 5), and the selected scalar is k = 6. G belongs to the curve because 5² mod 17 = 8 and (1³ + 7) mod 17 = 8. The toy public key is the point Q = kG. Scalar multiplication means adding G to itself k times using the group law; it does not mean multiplying each coordinate by k.

Point Addition

For P = (x₁, y₁) and Q = (x₂, y₂), the slope λ is calculated as follows. Division means multiplication by a modular inverse: a value whose product with the denominator is 1 modulo 17. [1]

P ≠ Q: λ = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁ mod 17 P = Q: λ = 3x₁22y₁ mod 17

x₃ = λ2 − x₁ − x₂ (mod 17) y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁ (mod 17)

O is the point at infinity and the identity element: O + P = P. When Q = −P, their sum is O. Doubling a point with y = 0 also gives O. These cases are handled before attempting to compute a modular inverse.

Distribution of Points and Coordinates

For G = (1, 5), each point in the sample occurs once. The possible x values are 1, 2, 5 and 12, each occurring twice. The y values are 5, 10, 9, 1, 16, 8, 7 and 12, each occurring once. Point frequencies are uniform over the enumerated domain, but coordinate frequencies are not uniform over all 17 field values.

Curve points satisfy an algebraic constraint, and their coordinates are not independent. In particular, P and −P have the same x coordinate. In the chosen encoding, x is always less than 16, so its most significant bit is always zero. This explains part of the observed bit imbalance.

Periodicity and Correlations

After 6G = (5, 8), the next points are 7G = (2, 7), 8G = (1, 12), 9G = O and 10G = G. The sequence repeats every 9 scalars. Over the range 1 to 36, there are 4 results equal to O; these are counted separately from coordinate statistics. O is not a valid public key.

Zero linear correlation between k and x does not establish independence. The exact relations Q(k + 1) = Q(k) + G and Q(k + ord(G)) = Q(k) still hold. On the canonical domain from 1 to ord(G) − 1, scalars correspond bijectively to the nonidentity points of the subgroup.

Avalanche Behavior and Cryptographic Meaning

The one-bit test enumerates all distinct unordered pairs of valid keys differing in one input bit. The output is encoded as two 5-bit coordinates, and the Hamming distance is counted across their 10 bits. With the required base point, an average of 4.889 output bits change. This result depends on the curve, domain and encoding; it does not establish cryptographic security.

The central security property of k → kG is the hardness of the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem, ECDLP. This small curve instead allows immediate exhaustive search. Cryptographic hashes accept variable-length messages and produce fixed-length digests; they require preimage, second-preimage and collision resistance. Bit diffusion and regular histograms are useful observations, but cannot replace cryptanalysis.
